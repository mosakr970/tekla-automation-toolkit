01 — the input

A tendon arrives as a column of numbers

The designer sends one Excel sheet per beam position. Cell A1 names the position. Each tendon is a group of three columns, Xn Yn Zn, with one row per station until X runs out.

What the columns mean
ColumnMeasured
XAlong the beam, from its start face. The beam must be modelled from PA to PG. On a polybeam, along the chord from its first to its last point.
YAcross the width, from the beam's centre, positive to the left looking from start to end.
ZUp from the bottom face directly below the point, to the underside of the tendon. These are the handle points.

By hand, that is a poly beam per tendon and per duct with every point typed in, in the beam's own coordinates. On a level box that is tedious. On these beams — sloping, rotated about their own axis, some of them polybeams whose soffit changes slope part-way along — it is easy to get slightly wrong, and a tendon 25 mm off is not visible until someone sections it.

02 — running it

Scan, assign, insert — and the window stays open

  1. Scan

    Lists the concrete beams, polybeams and slabs that are the main part of their cast unit, whose assembly prefix starts with the prefix field. The selection when there is one, the whole model otherwise. One row per position — the EK_MARK UDA, or the assembly position when that is empty — with a beam count.

  2. Give each position its sheet

    Browse per row, or From folder, which matches file names to positions: JPT-104.xlsx goes to JPT-104. The row shows the sheet's tendon count, in red if it cannot be read or its A1 names another position. A position left without a file gets nothing.

  3. Set the tendon and the duct

    Profile and material from the Tekla catalog, the last five used on top, anything typable — parametric profiles such as BL65*16 included. Name, class, numbering, and the duct's offset from the tendon. The duct is optional.

  4. Insert

    Every ticked position with a sheet, every beam of it. The parts go into each beam's cast unit as a steel sub-assembly, or in as loose parts.

  5. Read the results

    One row per beam: inserted, skipped because it already holds tendons, no Excel file, or failed with the reason. Double-click to select and zoom. The window stays open for the next set.

Settings, recent profiles and materials, and each position's sheet are kept per model, in InsertTendons.settings in the model folder.

03 — the hard part

What X, Y and Z mean on this beam

Placing a point is arithmetic once the frame is right. Every bug in the build was a wrong frame, and each came from trusting something Tekla hands over.

  • A beam's local Y is not “up”. It follows the Rotation setting. At TOP it points up. At FRONT it is horizontal, and the tendons came out lying on their side.
  • Pure vertical is not “up” either. Forcing global Z fixed FRONT and threw away JPT-107's −2.89° rotation. The height axis is whichever of the beam's own local Y and Z points most nearly upward, with its tilt kept and squared to the length.
  • A polybeam's own system follows its first segment only. Along the length, X is the chord from the first to the last point.
  • “At depth” is judged against the work plane. Depth FRONT puts a profile's lower face on its handle points only while the work plane's Z is up. So the parts are inserted in a work plane built on the sheet's frame, and the sheet's numbers go in exactly as written.
The bug the maths did not have

JPT-104's tendons came out running along global X, from the beam's side face. A probe macro replayed the tool's frame maths on the real beam and got them right.

The fault was upstream. A polybeam carries its points on the object, fetched in whatever work plane the view had at the time, and those were then used as if they were global. A straight beam asks Tekla for its axis each time, so it was never affected. Every beam is now re-read in the global plane before its frame is built.

04 — measuring

From the right surface, at the right height

  • Z follows the soffit point by point. At each station a vertical line is cast through the beam's solid and its lowest hit is the bottom face there. A polybeam's changing slope and a sloping soffit are followed without knowing anything about the shape.
  • Cuts are ignored. The solid is read without them, so a dapped end or a recess does not lift a tendon.
  • X starts at the face, at the tendon's own height. The first version took X = 0 at the rearmost point of the solid. On JPT-104, whose first segment slopes more than its chord, the end face is tilted in that frame, and every tendon started 2.79 mm outside the concrete. The face is now found by casting along the length at each tendon's first point.
Knowing what is already there

The first duplicate check looked for a tendon whose first point lay inside the beam. JPT-107 starts every tendon a metre outside it, so its beams were filled a second time.

The check now takes the middle point of every tendon and duct in the model, once per run, transforms it into each beam's frame and tests it against the beam's box. It works for sub-assemblies and loose parts alike.

06 — limitations

What it does not do

  • Straight segments between stations geometry

    A soffit whose slope changes between two rows is not followed there. Add a row at that station.

  • Bends in plan are not followed geometry

    A polybeam is assumed straight in plan. Only its bends in elevation are followed.

  • Beam direction decides X = 0 silent

    X runs from the start face, so the beam must be modelled from PA to PG. A beam modelled the other way gets its profile mirrored, with no warning.

  • The duplicate check is by name by design

    A beam holding parts named like the current tendon or duct is skipped. A second set under a different name is allowed on purpose — that is how more tendons are added to a beam.

  • Beams are found by assembly prefix silent

    Which requires numbering to be up to date. A beam never numbered is not found, and the A1 check compares against a stale position.

  • The window blocks while inserting interface

    Every Tekla call runs on the window's thread, so it is unresponsive during a scan or an insert. Deliberate: inserting moves the work plane, and the Open API is not thread-safe.

07 — time

What it saves

The macro's side is one session over the whole project set, setup included. The manual side is an estimate, and is marked as one.

With the macro ~5 min 59 beams in 17 positions, from opening the macro to the results: Scan, assigning the files, setting the profiles, Insert. 424 parts — 212 tendons and 212 ducts — and 11,282 points, none skipped, none failed. Almost all of it is a person filling in the window.
Estimated ~40 hours The team's estimate for modelling the same set by hand. The positions differ mostly in slope and orientation, so each beam's 190-odd points are placed in a frame of its own.
Per revision one run A revised sheet is a delete and a re-run. By hand, it is most of the original work again.

The honest framing

Five minutes against forty hours is the comparison, but only one side of it is a stopwatch. The forty hours is the team's estimate, so no multiplier is quoted. What the numbers show beyond doubt is where the cost goes: the job becomes preparing correct sheets, which has to happen either way.

The larger saving is the one that repeats. Every design revision used to mean retyping a position; now it is a re-run. And the frame, the fall and the soffit are handled by geometry rather than by the person typing, which is where the millimetres used to go.

08 — build notes

How it is put together

  • One file. All 1,445 lines travel as a single macro in XS_MACRO_DIRECTORY\modeling. Tekla compiles it at startup.
  • Written to a pre-C#6 subset, matching the other macros in the repository: no interpolation, no null-conditional, no null-coalescing.
  • Styled with HC Check — the same flat palette, hairline cards and tab bar, so the macros read as one toolset.
  • Debugged by probe. Each geometry bug was settled by a throwaway read-only macro, run through the MCP server's run_macro, writing the beam's frame, box and the tendons' points to a text file. It never ships.
  • Compiled before it is run. Every change was built against the framework C# compiler and the Tekla 2023 DLLs, and the Excel reader tested against the sample sheets, before a round trip through the model.
