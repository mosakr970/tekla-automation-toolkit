01 - the constraint

A hollow-core panel cannot be sawn to any width

The saw has to land between the cores rather than through a web. So a factory catalog does not offer a range of widths - it offers a handful of discontinuous bands, with dead ground in between. Openings carry their own limits, and those limits change depending on where the opening sits on the panel.

Checking that by hand means opening every panel, measuring it, and comparing against a catalog table. On a model with 900 panels nobody does that. It gets spot-checked, and the misses surface at the factory.

02 - what it reports

Seven findings, and the four zones a cut can sit in

Finding codes
CodeMeaning
width_out_of_rangeA cut panel's width matches none of the allowed bands for its profile. A panel wider than the delivered width also lands here.
varying_widthWidth is not constant along the length, so no single band applies. Sampled away from cuts reaching a side face, so notches at the supports do not trigger it.
cut_too_longAn opening exceeds the max length allowed for its zone and position.
cut_too_wideAn opening exceeds the max width allowed for its zone and position.
rib_partially_cutA cut goes part way through a rib instead of taking it away whole or leaving it alone. Applies to openings inside the panel and to notches cutting in from a side face alike.
no_width_windowsA cut panel was found, but its profile has no width bands entered.
no_rulesThe profile has no ranges at all. Unreachable from the window or the tool, which skip unlisted profiles - it appears only if the rule logic is called directly.

Cuts are classified on two axes - along the span and across the width - which gives four limit rows per profile, each with its own maximum length and width:

edge - reaches a side face mid - clear of both side faces Support cut
reaches a panel end support_edge800 × 450 mm support_mid800 × 500 mm Mid span cut
does not mid_span_edge1500 × 430 mm mid_span_mid1500 × 450 mm

Limits shown are HCE265 from the sample catalog. A blank cell means the catalog sets no limit there.

The distinction that keeps the noise down

A cut that does not go all the way through the panel is a surface recess, not an opening - the top-layer slots over the cores that take the ring-beam tie bars, for instance. By default those are skipped rather than measured against the opening limits.

In the measured run that was 639 of 742 cuts. Without that rule the report would have been nine parts noise. They are never hidden, but neither are they counted as checked: the Cuts column counts only the cuts actually judged, the status line totals the skipped ones, and the report file carries both per panel.

Cores and ribs

A rib must be left whole or taken away whole. Each cut is measured across the width, and each of its two boundaries either lands in a core, taking the ribs beside it away whole or leaving them alone, or lands inside a rib, leaving a sliver standing that carries nothing. The second case is rib_partially_cut.

There is no number to look up and none to enter. The cores are read off the panel solid, so the section's own geometry is the rule. The only setting is Flag part-cut ribs, beside Ignore partial-depth cuts under the cut limits, on by default.

It agrees with the width bands for a reason. On the TAM22 sheet the panel is 1196 wide over six 120 cores, with 69 ribs between them, and its cut bands (255-375, 444-564, 633-753, 822-942, 1010-1130) are precisely the spans of cores 2 to 6. The gaps between the bands are precisely the ribs. A catalog's cut widths are the positions at which a saw splits no rib.

03 - the hard part

Measuring a panel's width is not trivial

The rules engine is the easy half. Reading a reliable width off a Tekla panel is where the work went.

  • The width is not in the profile. A panel sawn narrower is routinely narrowed by a boolean cut, not by a profile change. Read the profile and every narrowed panel reports its full delivered width.
  • The bounding box lies the other way. Measure the cut solid's box and an end notch makes the whole panel read narrow.
  • So the panel is sampled. Intersection lines are cast across the panel at interior stations along the span, taking the outermost hits. An interior opening only adds hits between them, so it cannot affect the answer. The widest width still comes from the bounding box - no opening can widen a panel.
  • Height matters. At mid-thickness the sawn edge is an open void a line never reaches, and the side faces are shear-keyed, so they are recessed towards mid-height. Only near a flange is the section solid right across. Each station samples several heights and keeps the widest.
  • Cut-outs at supports are not taper. A panel with a notch at each end is still a constant-width panel, so stations falling inside an edge-reaching cut are dropped. Where every station falls inside one, the width comes from the bounding box alone.
  • Cores and ribs come from the one height width avoids. A line cast at mid-thickness, where the cores are widest, crosses the walls in pairs, and the pairs are the ribs. An odd count means the cast grazed a face, so that station is dropped. Stations inside a cut are skipped, and of the rest the most common rib count wins, because cores are routinely plugged at the panel ends.
  • Which axis is the width is decided from the uncut blank, whose section is the full delivered width by the panel thickness. That stays unambiguous even for a panel sawn narrower than it is thick, and it means panels modelled as beams and as slabs both work without configuration.
The bug that cost an afternoon

The very first solid-intersection call in a process returns zero hits. No exception, no error - just nothing. One lost sample dropped a station to the next-widest height, and a perfectly good 1200 mm panel was reported as 1169-1200, width not constant. Retrying the cast once removes it.

Everything is measured in the panel's own local coordinates, with the work plane set per panel and restored afterwards. Cutting parts are clipped to the panel body first, since they are normally modelled oversized to guarantee a clean cut.

04 - running it

One file in, one ranges file out

Delivery routes
RouteWho it is for
The macroDrop HC Check.cs into XS_MACRO_DIRECTORY\modeling and restart Tekla. It then sits in Applications & components as HC Check - no installer, no command line, no runtime to match.
The windowNon-modal and topmost, so Tekla stays usable while it is open - which matters, because double-clicking a result row selects and zooms that panel in the model.
The self-testBuilt into the same file, needs no model, and reports a pass or fail count. It replaces the pytest suite the earlier implementation had, because a macro has no CI, no linter and no type checker behind it.

  1. Scan the model

    Finds the panels by assembly prefix and lists every profile they use, with a count. Profiles not seen before are added with empty ranges.

  2. Enter the ranges

    Per profile: the delivered full width, the permitted width bands, the four cut limit rows, and the two switches for partial-depth cuts and part-cut ribs. Plus four shared tolerances - width, cut size, face reach (which decides a cut's category) and full depth (which decides whether a cut counts as an opening at all).

  3. Save

    Writes hc_check.json beside the macro, falling back to a per-user folder when the macro folder cannot be written to. Unknown keys are rejected rather than ignored, so a typo fails loudly instead of quietly dropping a limit. A fresh install is seeded from hc_check.sample.json, which carries real HCE profile values rather than placeholders.

  4. Run the check

    Measures the panels, fills the results table, colours the failures red. Double-click a row to select and zoom that panel in Tekla. Nothing is written to the model - the colour is a temporary state, and Clear colours removes it.

  5. Read the Log

    Its first line says how many panels had their ribs checked and how many did not, on a clean run too, so a quiet run cannot hide one that checked nothing. Below it, whatever went wrong is grouped and counted, with the panels named by assembly mark. Write report dumps every measured number, including each panel's core count and the ribs each cut severs or leaves part-standing.

05 - limitations

What it does not do

Taken from the code and the README rather than from intent. Several of these are deliberate.

  • Only widths, cut sizes and part-cut ribs are enforced rule scope

    Deliberately not checked: the maximum number of ribs one opening may sever, the minimum clear distance between openings, the maximum openings per panel, and the critical support length. Those come from a catalog's opening tables rather than from the section, so there is nothing in the model to derive them from.

  • A profile modelled without its cores is skipped for ribs geometry

    A solid section has no cores to find, so the rib check is skipped rather than failed. The Log's first line counts those panels and its RIBS NOT CHECKED group names them.

  • An unconfigured profile is skipped, not failed silent

    Nothing tells you a profile is unconfigured except a count in the status line. In the measured run that was 50 of 901 panels - about 6% of the model passed through untouched. Read the status line after every run.

  • Only boolean cuts are measured rule scope

    Polygon cuts, line cuts, fittings and chamfers have no cutting part and are invisible to the check. If a narrowing was not made with a boolean, the panel reports its full width.

  • Panels are assumed prismatic on local X geometry

    A curved or twisted panel is not handled meaningfully. If a blank's section is near-square, width and thickness cannot be told apart - the wider axis is used and a near-square blank warning goes to the Log under WIDTH FELL BACK TO THE BOUNDING BOX.

  • Taper is sampled, not traced geometry

    A width change confined to a short stretch between stations can be missed, and a panel whose cuts cover every station is judged on its bounding box alone, so a genuine taper there goes unseen.

  • Panels are found by assembly prefix silent

    Which requires numbering to be up to date. A panel that has never been numbered is not found at all. A mark ending in (?) is Tekla telling you that panel's numbering is stale.

  • Nothing durable is written to the model volatile

    The red is a Tekla temporary state. Any view redraw, filter change or model reopen drops it, and the results live only as long as the window stays open. Note the failures before closing it - or simply re-run.

  • The window is fixed size and blocks while running interface

    It cannot be resized; what overflows scrolls instead. Tekla calls run on the main thread, so the window is unresponsive during a scan or a check. That is deliberate: the Open API is not thread-safe and the check moves the work plane per panel, so a background thread could corrupt the geometry being read.

  • One profile edited at a time interface

    Ranges cannot be copied between profiles, and Scan re-adds profiles you removed.

  • The rule half is fully self-tested; the measuring half is not by design

    The verdict logic touches no Tekla API, so the built-in self-test exercises all of its cases with no model open and no licence in use. The measuring can only be verified against a live model, by running it - a macro has no CI to run it in.

06 - time

What it saves

Both sides are measured. Times measured on 2026-10-06 in Tekla Structures 2025 on a live project model, by running the macro's own code with a stopwatch from outside Tekla, at least twice - so they cover the macro's work, not the clicks in its window. The time by hand was taken by a detailer on the same model.

Measured 851 panels One run, every panel measured and every one of its 742 cuts classified - then checked panel by panel against a second independent implementation of the same rules, with zero disagreements. This is the claim that is actually evidenced.
By hand ~4.4 hours 204 panels were checked by hand in about an hour - about 18 seconds each to open, measure and compare against the catalog table. At that rate the 897 panels of the model take about 4.4 hours.
With the macro 38-52 s All 897 panels of the same model, over four runs - about 40 to 55 ms a panel. Almost all of it is the measuring. Finding the panels takes about 3 seconds, and the verdicts and colouring well under one.
Per revision one run And it is never a one-off. Every design revision needs the whole model checked again, which is where the manual version quietly stops happening.
One-off cost ~1 hour Entering a factory catalog's bands and cut limits per profile, once. Reused by every model using that catalog.

The honest framing

The hours saved are the weaker claim, because on a 900-panel model nobody was doing the manual pass in the first place. What actually changes is that a check which was previously spot-sampled becomes exhaustive - every panel, every revision.

No count of failing panels is quoted here on purpose. The measured run used the worked example shipped with the tool, not a real factory catalog, so its failure count describes the example rather than the model. The counts above describe the measuring, which is independent of whichever catalog is loaded. Run it against your own catalog before putting a failure count in front of anyone.

The value sits in where a failure gets caught. A width that no saw can land on is found in the model, in one run, instead of on the factory floor, where it becomes a redesign, a delayed delivery, or a panel remade.

What it does not save: entering the catalog ranges, and reading the findings. Reviewing the flagged panels is real work - it is just work on the handful flagged rather than on all 851.

07 - build notes

How it is put together

  • One file, by requirement. The tool is handed to people with no repository, no Python and no permission to run an executable, so the whole tool travels as a single macro. Tekla compiles it at startup.
  • Written to a pre-C#6 subset. No string interpolation, no null-conditional, no null-coalescing - matching what Tekla's macro compiler reliably accepts. Every number is formatted with InvariantCulture, because Tekla runs in locales where the decimal separator is a comma, and a stray comma would corrupt both the messages and the ranges file.
  • Ranges are data, not code. hc_check.json is re-read on every run, so edits made outside the window take effect without restarting anything. Nothing about a manufacturer is hardcoded.
  • Read-only in fact. Nothing is written to the model. The red on a failing panel is temporary display state, not a stored attribute, so a run leaves the model exactly as it found it.
